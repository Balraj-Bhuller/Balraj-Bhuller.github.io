Project Overview & Objectives

Soapomorph

The Soapomorph is an interactive, horror-themed soap dispensing system designed specifically for use in immersive horror theme park environments.

The primary goal of the Soapomorph is to blend functionality with entertainment, reinforcing the theme park's narrative even in transitional spaces such as bathrooms.

The Soapomorph is intended to be safe, durable, easy to maintain, and disassemble. Please note, this is only a prototype.

Build Process & Mission Log
PHASE 01 19/01/2026 - 18/02/2026

Initial Design

This was the first phase of the project. The group had been established prior in December but the initial steps of proposals and designs had started at this date. The projects we had in mind were: Windmill, Drone, Octopus, Butterfly, Jurassic Park Dinosaur, and Xenomorph. After a discussion we decided to proceed with project Soapomorph - a Soap dispenser using a Xenomorph and Warhammer Skeletons as a base, designed for use in theme parks.

The discussions of the first ways of creating a model came up. Discussed were foam sheets, Periastalic pumps, a track motion for the inner head, and starting designs were created via paint and reference pictures. The group decided on a stand alone base rather than key holes, as there were concerns about soap damaging the electronics which would be saved in the head and hence moved to a base below the head.

An early design of a track was also decided, with mechanical joint of gears selected at this stage. For developing designs, initial thoughts were to use 3D scanning, haptic pen and Blender to create this project. Early iterations of a Gantt chart and Moscow matrix were made. Two main design approaches were brought forward: a gear system to open the jaw with a track for the tongue extension, or using the jaw and skull as a base and connecting the tongue so it automatically extends when the jaw opens. A linear actuator option was quickly dismissed due to cost constraints.

Stakeholders began to be identified, materials were selected, a risk assessment was created, and the Test Plan was assigned to new members that joined the team.

PHASE 02 17/03/2026 - 14/04/2026

3D Modelling & Digital Prototyping

At the start of this phase, an early design of the skull had been created. New iterations of how the design would support given assembly and disposability were described at this stage too. As the skull needed headspace, the group decided that the top of the skull should be detachable for access to electronics and tubes for cleaning and maintenance purposes. The design team also printed off a Xenomorph tongue to show the scale and how the tongue would be fastened to the skull.

After a few discussions and iterations, a final design of the skull was brought forward. The design supports the idea of a headspace which will be held together by magnets. However, there was no mechanism designed at this stage yet. The only thing produced and showable was the skull and some documents. Hence began the design of the mechanism, the lid and the jaw.

Due to time constraints and the ambitiousness of the project, the next iterations of the skull would be needed to take forward. The possibility of doing small refinements and changes would still be there but nothing major about the skull wouldn't be able to be changed. In addition to this, due to the spring holidays coming up, some facilities and team members would not be reachable during this period.

PHASE 03 21/04/2026 - 29/04/2026

Final 3D Design Refinement

The group finished the final 3D design that is going to be printed. The problem at this stage was that the mechanism wasn't finished yet. The plan was to print this iteration of the skull and then figure out how to implement the mechanical joint from there. The prototype of the base had been brought and built with scrap wood, with a hinge door for refilling soap. The design of the base is as discussed in Phase 2 with some minor changes.

The Final 3D Design then also received multiple iterations to be compatible with the base. The group added a theme-appropriate, tendril-like throat which connected to a bracket. This bracket has a hole in it, and the tendrils also support the tubing from the soap to the head.

PHASE 04 30/04/2026 - 14/05/2026

Manufacturing Attempt

The first attempt to get the artefact printing ended in failure. Roughly 30% through the print, issues with the PP filament came up. These were related to the age of the PP as it was in storage for an unknown prolonged time. The exact issues are not known to the group. The group then attempted to get a second print approved for budgeting however this ended in failure too. As a last resort, the group booked another printing with their own finances, but even with printing in PLA, due to the late submission of the print, the artefact would not get ready before the deadline.

The 3D printing team informed the group that the current way of printing the entire artefact would not work properly. The group needed to slice the 3D model into smaller parts and find a way to connect those. The solution was to use simple small holes which align with the other parts just like Lego.

At this stage, the group finalized the tongue mechanical joint. The idea is that when the jaw opens, it drives the tongue forward like a piston. The same is true for when the jaw closes. This hinders the tongue from getting stuck between the jaw and skull.

FINAL NOTES

Project Summary: This project shows the iterative nature of prototype development and the importance of adaptability in design. The transition from the initial design to a servo-driven piston mechanism showcased how practical constraints and team collaboration refine ideas into solutions. The shift from a wall-mounted to a standalone design was driven by electronic protection concerns, illustrating how real-world requirements directly influence engineering decisions.

Manufacturing Lessons: The manufacturing phase revealed lessons about design for manufacturability. The PP filament failure highlighted the importance of material storage and handling, which is an often overlooked detail but essential to success. Due to time constraints, PLA was used to attempt to speed up the printing process in an attempt to get a physical artefact before deadlines. The subsequent reading into modular segmentation transformed potential project failures into a valuable learning experience about optimizing designs for 3D printing capabilities.

What Worked Well: Hand sketching effectively pre-explored the design space before committing to CAD, preventing false starts. The magnetic housing concept proved elegant and provides maintenance access without fasteners. Building a wooden base prototype early showed integration issues before the full 3D design had been modelled. Blender's flexibility allowed transitions between artistic and mechanical designs. Team communication through annotated sketches kept all members aligned despite project complexity.

What Could Be Improved: More testing of materials could have prevented PP filament degradation issues. Physical prototype testing of the jaw mechanism, even a cardboard mockup, would have validated the piston concept before depending entirely on animations. Design should have started from the mechanism first, then built the skull around it, improving efficiency and providing scale earlier.

If We Started Again: We would start the artefact from the mechanism first, rather than designing the skull aesthetics and fitting mechanics afterwards. Building a working jaw mechanism prototype early, testing the servo range, force requirements, and position dynamics physically, would have provided a foundation for all design.

Key Learnings: Design for manufacturability is as critical as design for function. Mechanism design should be foremost in design, and digital simulation must be validated against physical reality.
